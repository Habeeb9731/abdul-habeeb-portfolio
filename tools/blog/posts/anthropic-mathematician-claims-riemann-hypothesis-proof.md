Levent Alpöge had a busy week. His employer, Anthropic, became the first publicly traded frontier AI lab. In the same week, [according to TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/20/ai-claimed-a-solution-to-the-167-year-old-riemann-hypothesis-and-a-human-did-too/), he announced that he appears to have solved the Riemann hypothesis, a conjecture from 1859 and one of the Millennium Prize problems.

In an essay, Alpöge wrote that "the precise timing is horrible." He says he messaged Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei the previous Sunday night to ask how to announce the result. The claim is his, and it has not been independently settled.

## What has been checked

The result is described as a Lean-compilable solution. Lean is an open-source language for writing mathematical proofs as code, so a compiled proof has passed a machine check. TechCrunch reports that several people in a Zulip chat for mathematicians, including MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland, say they have successfully compiled Alpöge's solution. The article was updated to say the Lean proof has been checked by independent sources.

Compilation is not the same as understanding. The mathematical argument itself is still being worked through. TechCrunch quotes a comment from the chat that is dense enough that the article's author says they could not follow it. The article says it will take a while before the community has a handle on the proof, and that it is too early to say whether anyone understands it.

## Alpöge's account

Alpöge said he felt somewhat guilty about releasing the proof without a human-readable explanation. He also said his long interest in the problem meant he "could not live" if he waited to release it. He described a "stream-of-consciousness jaunt" through finding the argument's pieces "through incredible confusion with Claude," and wrote that the model "flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line."

He has committed to working toward a human-intelligible explanation. Alpöge and NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster also released another proof, related to the Navier-Stokes equations. TechCrunch notes that these problems helped start a race this summer between frontier labs and mathematicians.

## A human proof in the same week

Stanford professor Ravi Vakil posted a proof of the General Riemann hypothesis by Stanford postdoc Alessandro Masullo. Vakil wrote that "it is all human, with no AI." He said the plan had been to bring Masullo to Stanford for a year to polish the writing before announcing it, but that "now it seems best to put out the current draft." Masullo says the work took 12 years.

## My read

The headline claim is that an AI-assisted proof exists. What is verified so far is that the Lean code compiles, which is a real check. Whether the formal statement is the one the field cares about, and whether the argument holds up, are separate questions that will take people time.

I'd also keep in mind that the author works for the company whose tools helped, and whose CEO he contacted about the announcement. The article doesn't report any comment from Anthropic. For now, I'd treat Alpöge's account as a serious claim under review, not a settled result. The Masullo proof is a useful reminder that the field's work is not waiting on AI.
