Several large US banks now say that if you give a third-party AI tool access to your bank account, you carry the risk. [TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/18/more-banks-says-customers-using-ai-agents-are-on-their-own-even-if-they-get-hacked/) that Citibank changed its terms of service this week to say so.

## What the banks changed

AI agents can be given credentials for bank and brokerage accounts and other financial portals. The idea is that they can analyze spending, offer investment advice, and handle transactions and bill payments. Banks are not comfortable with that. According to TechCrunch, they are telling customers they won't be held responsible for reversing an intentional or unintentional transaction made by an automated assistant.

Citi's update goes further. It says that if an AI tool leaks a customer's credentials or is hacked, the customer is responsible for anything hackers do with the account. A Citi spokesperson told TechCrunch: "As third-party AI tools become more widely available, we want customers to understand the risks associated with sharing banking credentials or authorizing those tools to access their accounts." The spokesperson also said customers who grant access to third-party AI agents "are responsible for activities conducted through those agents."

Citi's terms also note that AI agents Citi itself provides can be used without worrying about additional liability.

Other banks are doing similar things. TechCrunch reports that JPMorgan and Bank of America announced similar policies last month, and that PNC changed its terms too. Citizens emailed customers warning that they are also responsible for anything their AI assistant does, according to an email TechCrunch saw. The report doesn't say whether any of these banks have had a specific incident that prompted the change.

## Why this matters

The report points to two reasons. The first is security. Banks see AI tools as a new way in, and they are shifting the cost of a breach to the customer. The second is competition. TechCrunch notes that AI tools could help consumers avoid fees and low-interest deposit accounts, which the banks depend on for margins. If AI agents become a main way people manage money, banks want to control that path.

The contrast with AI startups is useful. Anthropic's personal finance tool, launched earlier this month, is billed as "read-only" and as something that "can't move, manage, or invest your money." That design avoids the liability question altogether.

## My read

Before you connect any AI tool to a bank or brokerage account, read the bank's current terms. Under these updates, a hack or a bad transaction made through an agent may be yours to absorb. Treat account credentials the way you would treat a password to your own vault. If a tool only needs to read your data to be useful, prefer read-only access. If it needs to move money, ask yourself whether you are comfortable carrying the loss.
