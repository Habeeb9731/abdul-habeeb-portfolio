Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney [announced an equity stake in Cohere](https://ca.news.yahoo.com/carney-announces-1-6b-federal-193921071.html) at the opening of the Canada-EU summit in Montreal, on or around 29 October.

## The deal

The federal investment of up to C$1.6 billion comes through the Canada Development Investment Corporation. It joins a $7 billion round led by Germany's Schwarz Group, the parent of Lidl and Kaufland, with Nvidia and Bell Canada also participating. That values Cohere at nearly $36 billion. The report doesn't state a currency for each figure, so I've left them as written.

In return, Cohere must:

- Keep its Canadian headquarters and incorporation.
- Create 1,000 new high-paying jobs.
- Spend at least $1.7 billion on Canadian computing infrastructure by 2030.
- Report progress to Ottawa.

"And now, Canada has a stake in it," Carney said. The article reports no criticism of the deal, so it mostly reflects the government's and the company's framing.

## Why it's interesting

A supermarket group leading an AI funding round is an unusual sentence. Add a national government as a shareholder, and it fits a wider pattern alongside [Mistral's European datacentres](/blog/mistral-large-4/) and [Aleph Alpha's Kolibri](/blog/aleph-alpha-kolibri/): countries and regions don't want to depend entirely on American or Chinese AI.

## My read

A government taking equity changes the relationship. It gets upside and influence, but also a reason to favour one company when it buys AI or writes rules. Canada's conditions are concrete, which is good. What I'd want to see next is how those commitments are checked, and whether Ottawa treats Cohere the same as its competitors in procurement.

It's worth noting that Cohere is also the company behind [the report on Qwen](/blog/open-weight-model-beijing-upload/), so sovereignty and security are becoming part of the same sales pitch.
