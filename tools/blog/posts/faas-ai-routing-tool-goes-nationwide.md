The US Federal Aviation Administration is expanding SMART, an AI tool that advises air traffic controllers, from the airspace over Washington, DC, to the airspace of all 22 of its centers. [Ars Technica reports](https://arstechnica.com/features/2026/11/faa-expands-ai-advice-to-manage-air-traffic-congestion-across-entire-us-airspace/) that airline executives support the rollout. The union representing air traffic controllers says it was not involved in designing or testing the system.

## What SMART does

According to the FAA's fact sheet, SMART is a cloud-based system. It combines "weather patterns, flight paths, traffic flow and controller staffing metrics" to predict air traffic patterns. It is designed to make scheduling recommendations, identify optimal flight routes, and determine the best order of arrivals for aircraft.

The FAA says a team of its specialists based in DC validates SMART's recommendations before they are passed to air traffic control facilities across the country.

## The numbers so far

SMART went into use in the National Capital Region on September 21. The FAA says it has helped reroute 180 flights since then, saving an average of 10 minutes and almost 80 gallons of fuel per flight. These are the agency's own figures. The FAA's statement doesn't say how the averages were calculated, and the sample is small and limited to one region.

The FAA put the expansion in its own words in a statement posted November 10: "After a successful initial rollout to the National Capital Region in September, the FAA will now use SMART in the airspace of all 22 centers across the country to reduce delays, reduce fuel consumption and improve the travel experience for the flying public."

The system is listed at $875 million.

## The labor question

The union representing air traffic controllers has said it was not involved in designing or testing SMART. No response from the union to the expansion has been published yet, and the FAA's statement doesn't address the union's concerns.

## My read

The expansion is a big commitment. The reported gains come from roughly seven weeks of use in one region, and they are the FAA's own numbers. The human review step is the most important safeguard in the FAA's description, because it means specialists in DC decide whether a recommendation goes to a facility. How that review works at scale, across 22 centers, is the detail to watch.

The union's objection matters for the same reason. Controllers are the people who will act on these recommendations, and their experience of the tool will decide whether it helps in practice. Airline support is a useful signal, but it doesn't answer whether the people running the airspace were consulted. Until the FAA and the union say more, the reasonable position is cautious interest: the system may well reduce delays, but the evidence so far is limited and the people most affected haven't been brought in.
